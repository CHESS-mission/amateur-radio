Amateur Radio & Open Telemetry

The EPFL Spacecraft Team is a student association. Our satellites are designed, built and operated by students as hands-on training in space engineering, and we want to share what we learn with the worldwide amateur radio community.

Our educational purpose

More than 100 students work with us to learn how a real spacecraft comes to life: from the satellite platform to the ground segment and the radio link. Operating in the amateur-satellite service is part of that training. Students learn about link budgets, modulation, protocols, antennas and satellite operations, in the open and alongside the amateur radio community.

Our goal is to give the next generation of Swiss space engineers practical experience, and to give something back to the community that has supported small satellites for decades.

Our ground segment

Our ground segment is installed on the highest roof of the EPFL campus. The antennas and the ground station software, which also serves as our mission control software, are largely built by students.

Our contribution to the amateur radio community

Open telemetry

Our goal is for the telemetry transmitted by our satellite to be received and decoded by radio amateurs around the world, so that anyone with suitable equipment can follow the mission.

SatNOGS network

We are part of the SatNOGS community and operate a UHF antenna as a ground station, taking part in the global effort to track and receive satellites.

Public telecommands

We plan to offer licensed radio amateurs a set of public, non-critical telecommands for radio communication experiments and spacecraft data requests. The full list is below.

Public telecommands

Once the spacecraft is in nominal operations, licensed radio amateurs will be able to send a set of predefined, non-critical commands to our UHF transceiver. They let you run radio communication experiments and request spacecraft data. Requests are subject to limits on frequency, response size and transmission time. The public commands are not available during the launch and early operations phase or during commissioning, roughly the first four weeks after launch.

The public interface cannot control the on-board computer, change spacecraft modes, configure the transceiver or run privileged commands. Mission-critical communications always have priority, and the half-duplex UHF transceiver cannot receive while it transmits.
